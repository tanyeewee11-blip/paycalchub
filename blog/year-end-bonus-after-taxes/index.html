Year-End Bonus After Taxes: What You Actually Take Home

Bonus & Pay June 2026 6 min read

A $10,000 year-end bonus sounds great. After the IRS takes its share — 22% federal supplemental withholding plus FICA — you keep about $7,035. Here’s the full table for common bonus amounts, why the withholding rate can feel aggressive, and what you can actually do about it.

Why Bonuses Are Withheld Differently Than Regular Pay

Bonuses are classified as supplemental wages by the IRS. For amounts under $1 million, employers typically use the flat 22% federal withholding rate — rather than the standard bracket-based method applied to your regular paycheck. This makes withholding simple and consistent, but it doesn’t necessarily match your actual marginal tax rate.

On top of the 22% federal withholding, FICA applies in full: 6.2% Social Security (up to the $184,500 wage base) plus 1.45% Medicare = 7.65%. If you’ve already passed the Social Security wage cap for the year, only the 1.45% Medicare portion applies to your bonus.

Withholding vs. Actual Tax — You May Get Some Back

The 22% withholding rate is just withholding — it’s not your final tax bill. At year-end, your bonus is combined with all your other income to determine your actual marginal rate. If your true marginal rate is below 22% (which is possible for workers in the 12% or lower brackets), you’ll receive the difference back as part of your tax refund. If your marginal rate is above 22% (24%, 32%, etc.), you’ll owe a bit more at filing time.

Example: You earn $50,000/year (12% bracket at the margin) and receive a $5,000 bonus. Your employer withholds $1,100 (22%) in federal tax on the bonus. At filing, the bonus is taxed at your actual marginal rate of 12%, meaning you owe only $600. You’ll get $500 back as part of your refund.

Can You Reduce the Tax on a Bonus?

A few legitimate strategies, in order of effectiveness:

  • Maximize pre-tax 401(k) or 403(b) contributions around the same time — If you have room left in your annual contribution limit ($23,500 for 2026 under age 50), you can elect to have more of each regular paycheck go to your 401(k), which reduces your taxable income at year-end and partially offsets the bonus income. You generally can’t direct a bonus itself into a 401(k) post-payment, but your overall contribution strategy matters.
  • HSA contribution if eligible — HSA contributions are pre-tax and reduce federal AGI.
  • Defer the bonus if possible — Some employers allow you to defer receipt of a bonus to the following January, which can shift the income into a different tax year (potentially useful if your income will be lower next year).
  • Accept the withholding, expect the refund — For most moderate-income workers, the 22% withholding simply means a larger refund in spring. No additional action needed.
⚠️ This covers federal withholding only. State income tax varies significantly — from 0% in no-tax states to 9%+ in California. Use the Salary Calculator for your state’s specific rates.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much of a bonus do you actually get after taxes? +
Using the flat supplemental withholding method, you keep about 70.3% of a bonus after the 22% federal withholding and 7.65% FICA. A $10,000 bonus nets approximately $7,035. State income tax reduces this further depending on where you live.
Why is my bonus taxed so heavily? +
Bonuses are treated as supplemental wages and withheld at a flat 22% federal rate (for amounts under $1 million), which is higher than the effective rate many workers pay on regular wages. However, this is withholding — not your final tax. If your actual marginal rate is lower than 22%, you may get some of it back as a refund when you file.
Can I reduce the tax on my bonus? +
You can reduce the amount of your bonus that is taxable by making pre-tax contributions to a 401(k) or HSA around the same time — but you can’t change the withholding rate on the bonus itself. The final tax owed depends on your marginal rate at year-end, not the withholding rate.
